02 · The Story
The number that
changed everything

For five years, I caved. Every time a client said "that's too expensive," I took it as a verdict on my worth — not as a negotiation tactic. And I'd give in. Every time. "We could do something simpler." But I'd still deliver the whole thing. Just to close the deal. Just to feel needed.

The hardest part to admit: I wasn't caving for the clients. I was caving because I needed their validation more than I needed their money.

Until one day I quoted 500% above my actual rate. My voice was shaking. They said no. But in that rejection, I discovered something unexpected: I could come down to 300% above. And that second offer closed. With a confidence I'd never felt before in any pricing conversation.

I realized: if it doesn't feel worth it to me, it won't feel worth it to anyone else. My language changed. My presentation changed. My proposals changed. I started asking myself: how does someone who bills what I want to bill look, talk, and carry themselves? And I built from there.

The Anchoring Effect — Tversky & Kahneman, 1974
The first number a person hears anchors all of their subsequent estimates. In a pricing negotiation, whoever speaks first sets the playing field. If that's you — and it's high — you've already won half the negotiation before it starts.
Tversky, A., & Kahneman, D. (1974). Science, 185(4157), 1124–1131.
Price as a signal of quality — Ariely et al., 2003
Dan Ariely proved at MIT that charging low literally makes your work appear worse. Participants reported objectively inferior results when they paid less for the same product. Your price doesn't just reflect your value — it constructs it.
Ariely, D., Loewenstein, G., & Prelec, D. (2003). Quarterly Journal of Economics, 118(1), 73–105.

04 · The System
Three layers.
One system.
01
The High Anchor
Quote first, and quote high. The first number defines the playing field. Galinsky & Mussweiler (2001) proved that whoever opens with high numbers closes more favorable deals — even against more experienced counterparts.
02
The Systemic Reframe
Shifting the question from "how much does it cost?" to "what's it costing you not to have this solved?" reframes the entire conversation. You're not defending a price — you're surfacing a cost that already exists.
03
Your House in Order
Invoice, contract, deck, website, language. This layer operates before you open your mouth. When your structure is solid, the client has already made up their mind before they hear the number.
Win Without Pitching — Blair Enns
"The biggest mistake creative professionals make isn't charging too little — it's entering the client's buying process instead of leading their own sales process. Whoever defines the rules of the game defines the price."
Enns, B. (2010). The Win Without Pitching Manifesto. RockBench Publishing.

05 · The Scripts
The exact
words

Each script works on three levels: it validates the client, shifts the frame from price to value, and holds your position without conceding.

When they say: "That's not in our budget"
A project can feel expensive when you look at it in isolation. But nothing in a business exists in a vacuum — everything connects. Would you let me take a quick look at your bigger picture? We might be able to scope this in a way that makes the investment feel right, right now.
When they say: "That's too expensive"
My question is: what's it costing you today to not have this solved? If what we build together improves how your market sees you and brings in better clients, the real cost is the cost of not doing it.
When they lowball you
I appreciate the offer, and I want to be straight with you — working within those terms would compromise the process that actually delivers the result you're looking for. What I can do is adjust the scope. But we'd need to be completely clear about what stays in and what comes out.
